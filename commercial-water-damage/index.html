Commercial Water Damage Restoration in Sachse, TX

Water in a commercial building raises questions a house does not, from permits to scheduling. We extract and dry the space and explain which steps go through the city.

A burst line, a failed sprinkler head or a roof leak over a business floor creates a clock. Water moves across an open floor plan, climbs into drywall and reaches the ceiling grid below. The faster it is mapped, the fewer materials need to come out.

This page describes what our crews do and the steps the city expects for commercial work. We do not list industries or past jobs here. If the water came from storm weather, see storm water damage.

How commercial water damage happens

Supply line failures, drain backups, roof leaks, HVAC condensate and storm water are the usual sources. In a commercial building, a leak can start in a space nobody visits, such as a mechanical room or a ceiling above a closed unit, and run for a while before anyone sees a stain.

The Census Bureau counts Sachse at 32,834 residents in 2025, up from 27,103 in 2020. A growing town means a mix of older and newer buildings, and each one handles water differently, which is why a technician starts with a walkthrough.

What you see and what it means

  • Stained or sagging ceiling tiles. Water has collected above the grid.
  • Wet carpet along a wall. A leak may be inside the wall base.
  • Peeling paint or bubbled drywall. Moisture is traveling through the board.
  • A musty smell after the building is closed. Moisture is trapped in a hidden area.
  • Water on a concrete floor with no clear source. A line under or inside a wall may be leaking.

First-hour safety steps

  • Close the water at the building main or the zone valve if you can reach it safely.
  • Keep people out of wet areas and block them with signs or tape.
  • Stay away from wet electrical equipment. Call 911 or the electric utility for any electrical risk.
  • Move inventory and records onto dry shelving and off the floor.
  • Call the building owner or manager, and photograph the damage before cleanup.
  • Call our line for extraction and drying.

How we handle the work

A technician maps the wet area with moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera, then sets a plan with the owner or manager. Standing water comes out with extractors sized to the space. Wet ceiling tile, insulation and base materials are removed when they cannot dry in place.

Air movers and low-grain refrigerant dehumidifiers dry the structure, and moisture readings are logged each visit. Work areas can be sealed off so the rest of the building keeps running. ANSI/IICRC S500, the water damage standard, is the reference for this work.

What changes the cost

The square footage that is wet, the floor and ceiling materials, how long the water sat, whether the work must happen after hours and how many days of drying the readings call for all change the job. Cleanup in an occupied building also takes more planning.

The scope of work and the price come first, and you decide whether to proceed.

Permits and the city

The City of Sachse requires a permit for construction or repair of any building under Sec. 3-5 of its code. For non-residential remodel work, the city asks for scaled plans and a sealed design professional when the work is 50,000 dollars or more.

It also asks for an asbestos survey on work that remodels an existing building and a certificate of occupancy application after completion. Development Services is at 469-429-4781.

Commercial water damage and your insurance

The Texas Department of Insurance explains that sudden and accidental water damage is generally covered and gradual leaks are not. Commercial policies differ in deductibles and in how business interruption is handled, so read yours.

Keep the written scope, photos and moisture readings. They support the claim whichever way it goes.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Waiting for the owner or manager to decide. Wet drywall and carpet get worse each day.
  • Running the HVAC through a wet zone. It can spread humidity to the rest of the building.
  • Pulling out ceiling tile without a plan. Some can be saved, and some wiring may be live.
  • Skipping documentation. Photos and readings matter for a claim.
  • Starting rebuild work before checking permits. It can delay the project.
