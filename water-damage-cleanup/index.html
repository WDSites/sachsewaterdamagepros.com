Wet carpet and soggy drywall: what cleanup covers

Most cleanup calls come from the same few discoveries. Carpet squishes underfoot. Baseboards are swollen. Insulation is wet inside the wall. A closet smells like a damp towel. Cleanup deals with the leftovers that remain after the water has been stopped.

That means removing what cannot be saved, cleaning and sanitizing what can, and handling odor at its source. It also means documenting what was removed so a later repair or an insurance claim has a clear record.

When cleanup cannot wait

  • The water was sewage or floodwater. Contaminated porous materials should be removed promptly.
  • The loss happened days ago. Materials that sat wet for a long time are harder to save.
  • There is a sour or musty smell. Odor usually means moisture is still trapped somewhere.
  • A family member has an event or guests are coming. Do not rush a surface fix over wet material.
  • Contents are stacked on wet floors. Items pull moisture upward and stain as they sit.

The first 48 hours: what you can do yourself

You can help in the first hours without making things worse. Pick up small items from wet floors and place aluminum foil or plastic under furniture legs. Open closet doors, remove drawers so air reaches the cabinet, and run the home's air conditioning if the power in that area is safe.

Do not walk on wet carpet with dirty shoes, use a household vacuum on water or turn on ceiling fixtures under a wet ceiling. Do not paint or caulk over stains, because that traps moisture. If water came from a washer, dishwasher or toilet, treat it as contaminated and wear gloves.

What a crew removes and what it keeps

Technicians sort materials into three groups: dry and keep, dry in place with equipment, and remove. Carpet pad is the most commonly discarded item because it holds water and cannot be sanitized. Drywall that has wicked water more than a short distance is cut out along a straight line so repair is simple.

Baseboards, trim and cabinet toe kicks are often removed so air can reach the wall cavity. After removal, hard surfaces are cleaned with antimicrobial products appropriate to the water category. The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard guides which materials may be dried and which should be discarded. Any company can tell you how it applies that standard in plain words.

Odor, sanitizing and deodorizing

Sanitizing is not the same as drying. It addresses bacteria on surfaces after Category 2 or 3 water, and it happens after contaminated material is removed. Deodorizing addresses the smell that lingers in the structure.

A true fix for odor is finding and drying the moisture. Masking agents only cover what is still wrong. Technicians check the odor source with moisture readings and thermal imaging, then clean and dry the area before using any deodorizing product.

How cleanup connects to the rest of the job

Cleanup sits between two other stages. Before it comes emergency water removal, which pulls standing water out. During it, water mitigation dries the structure. After it, water damage repair rebuilds what was removed.

If you are not sure which stage you are in, call and describe what you see. Dispatch can tell you which step fits.

Cost factors for cleanup

The scope depends on the water category, the amount of debris and contents involved, the area of flooring and drywall removed, whether odor treatment is needed and how much disposal is involved. A clean water cleanup of one room costs far less than a sewage cleanup through several rooms.
